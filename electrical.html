Where the work slips

You know these days.

The work is never the problem. It's everything that rings, pings and piles up while you're doing it.

Sparks at the panel, voicemail on the line

Someone sees sparks or smells burning and calls the number on your van. The first thing they hear should be an instruction to get out and call 911, not a voicemail greeting.

Big bids that go cold

Panel upgrades, EV chargers and whole-home generators are big decisions. The homeowner who said they'd think about it signs with whoever checked back in.

Crew in the attic, phone in the van

Your crew is pulling wire in a Texas attic in July, and the office is one person. The builder who needs a rough-in next week gets voicemail and gives it to someone else.

What does Bea do if a caller describes something dangerous?

Safety comes before booking. For sparking, a burning smell, a hot panel or water near electrical, she tells the caller to get away from it and call 911, or the utility for a downed line, then alerts your on-call electrician. You and your Beekeeper decide what goes on that list.

Will Bea try to troubleshoot over the phone?

No. Beyond the safety steps in your playbook, like leaving a tripping breaker off, she doesn't diagnose anything. She books the visit and passes along what the caller told her.

Can Bea take calls from builders and general contractors?

Yes. She can take a request for rough-in or trim-out, note the job address and lot number, and send it to whoever runs your schedule.

Can Bea tell callers what a panel upgrade costs?

Only the ranges you've approved. Panel and generator prices depend on the house, so she books an estimate, and Miles follows up once it's sent.
